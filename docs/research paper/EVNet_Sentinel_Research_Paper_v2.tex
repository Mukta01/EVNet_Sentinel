\begin{abstract}
Electric vehicle charging stations are networked computers whose compromise can halt charging, metering and billing. We present EVNet Sentinel, a network intrusion detection system for the link between charging stations and their management system, and use it to reproduce and audit the online-learning detector of Makhmudov et al. on the CICEVSE2024 dataset. We find that the published preprocessing retains absolute capture timestamps: a decision tree given one timestamp column alone recovers the fifteen-class label with 99.76 percent accuracy, above the published 98.40 percent. We further show that the drift detector fires on the seams between concatenated capture files, and that the high accuracy of streaming evaluation in capture order is matched by simply repeating the previous label. With the leakage removed and every result averaged over three seeds, volumetric floods remain perfectly separable while the six nmap reconnaissance modes are not, and an information-theoretic analysis shows this ceiling is a property of the flow features rather than of model capacity. A per-attack test harness finds that no attack passes as normal traffic, that scans are recognised as reconnaissance but rarely named exactly, and that Random Forest offers the best naming with the fewest false alarms. Requiring confidence before naming an attack converts most wrong scan names into correct category alerts. The system is delivered with a prediction service, role-based response guidance and an interactive front end built on real held-out flows.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The transition to electric mobility has produced a large, interconnected cyber-physical infrastructure of Electric Vehicle Charging Stations (EVCS). A public charger is a networked computer that communicates over two standard protocols: ISO~15118 between the vehicle and the station (Electric Vehicle Supply Equipment, EVSE), and the Open Charge Point Protocol (OCPP) between the station and its Charging Station Management System (CSMS). The OCPP link carries authorisation, meter values and remote control, so disrupting it stops drivers from charging and operators from billing. These links expose charging networks to denial of service (DoS), reconnaissance, false-data injection and host-level compromise \cite{b1,b3}.

Machine-learning intrusion detection systems (IDS) trained offline cannot adapt when traffic changes, a phenomenon known as \emph{concept drift}. Makhmudov et al.\ \cite{b4} addressed this with the first online, streaming IDS for charging systems: an Adaptive Random Forest (ARF) \cite{b6} combined with ADaptive WINdowing (ADWIN) drift detection \cite{b7}, reporting 99.13\% binary and 98.40\% multiclass accuracy on the CICEVSE2024 dataset \cite{b8}.

This paper reproduces that work and asks whether those figures measure the detection of attacks or a property of how the dataset was assembled. We present \textbf{EVNet Sentinel}, a network IDS for the station-to-CSMS link, and use it as the vehicle for the audit. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Leakage audit.} Independently verifiable findings: timestamp leakage, a discarded legitimate feature, label autocorrelation in stream order, drift alarms that coincide with capture-file seams, and a residual port fingerprint (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}).
    \item \textbf{Corrected benchmarks} of four static classifiers and ARF+ADWIN, reported as mean and standard deviation over three seeds with macro-$F_1$ as the headline metric (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
    \item \textbf{An explanation of the reconnaissance ceiling} from the structure of the errors and a per-feature mutual-information analysis (Section~\ref{sec:why}).
    \item \textbf{Per-attack evaluation and model choice}: detection, categorisation and naming rates per attack and model, false-alarm rates, and a confidence-gated naming rule (Sections~\ref{sec:perattack}--\ref{sec:confidence}).
    \item \textbf{A provenance analysis of the testbed} showing that every attack targets a charging station and that the data were recorded passively (Section~\ref{sec:routes}).
    \item \textbf{A working system}: prediction service, role-based response guidance and an interactive front end (Section~\ref{sec:system}).
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:lit} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:problem} formalises the task, Section~\ref{sec:dataset} describes the data, Sections~\ref{sec:leakage}--\ref{sec:method} present the audit, system and method, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results, and Sections~\ref{sec:repro}--\ref{sec:conclusion} cover reproducibility, limitations and conclusions.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit}

This section reviews the five works that shaped EVNet Sentinel, states the gap each leaves, and compares them.

\subsection{Bean et al.\ (2026): EVSE cybersecurity survey \cite{b1}}

Bean et al.\ survey ML-based intrusion detection for EVSE across network, host and power-consumption monitoring. They formalise the IDS lifecycle as traffic capture, labelling, modelling, deployment and forensics, and review 24 studies on CICEVSE2024. They find that CICEVSE2024 is the dominant benchmark, that Random Forest and ensembles are strongest on flow data, and that concept drift, scalability and application-layer (OCPP message) monitoring remain open.

\textbf{Gap:} the survey takes reported accuracies at face value and does not audit the preprocessing behind them.

\subsection{Joglekar et al.\ (2026): hybrid NIDS and HIDS \cite{b2}}

Joglekar et al.\ combine a network IDS with a host IDS. Their network component, XGBoost on 547,834 CICEVSE2024 flows with 67 features, reaches 99.99\% accuracy; the host component reaches 96.60\% on host events and 70.34\% on power traces, and the hybrid 83.47\%. They remove IP, MAC and vendor identifiers to prevent leakage and show that flow-level analysis outperforms packet level.

\textbf{Gap:} timestamps are retained, so the 99.99\% is exposed to the capture-session fingerprint of Section~\ref{sec:leakage}; training is offline and cannot adapt to drift.

\subsection{Malimage et al.\ (2023): EV cybersecurity challenges \cite{b3}}

Malimage et al.\ analyse the problem from an industry and regulatory perspective: real breaches involving vehicle and charging platforms, the attack surface across grid, communication and user domains, weaknesses of OCPP and OCPI, and the relevant frameworks (NIST, ISO/SAE~21434, UN~R155). They note that routing many charge points through one location creates a single point of failure with grid-level consequences.

\textbf{Gap:} the paper motivates detection but proposes no detector.

\subsection{Makhmudov et al.\ (2025): online ML for EVCS IDS \cite{b4}}

This is the baseline we reproduce. ARF with 20 trees, a 50\% feature subset per split and ADWIN ($\delta = 0.002$) is run as a streaming learner on the CICEVSE2024 network traffic, reporting 99.13\% binary and 98.40\% multiclass accuracy, 12 and 11 drift events, and 3.7\,ms per instance. Drift handling is presented as the method's key advantage.

\textbf{Gap:} the preprocessing retains six absolute timestamp columns and discards \col{dst\_port}; evaluation in capture order is inflated by label autocorrelation; and the detected drift coincides with file seams (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}).

\subsection{Tanyildiz et al.\ (2025): RUL-GAN pre-warning \cite{b5}}

Tanyildiz et al.\ predict \emph{when} the next attack will occur rather than whether a flow is malicious. A GAN generates synthetic attack samples for LSTM, GRU, RNN, CNN and MLP regressors of the time to the next attack; the best (GAN-MLP) reaches $R^2 = 0.7379$ on the EVS One dataset.

\textbf{Gap:} a different dataset prevents direct comparison, and GAN-plus-deep-learning training is costly for real-time use.

\subsection{Research gaps addressed}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Unaudited preprocessing.} No prior work tests whether CICEVSE2024 accuracies are artefacts of capture-session leakage.
    \item \textbf{Single-seed evaluation.} With classes of 28 to 81 flows, single-seed results are unreliable; we report mean and standard deviation over three seeds.
    \item \textbf{Accuracy as the headline.} Accuracy is dominated by flood classes; macro-$F_1$ exposes the DoS-versus-reconnaissance gap.
    \item \textbf{No per-attack or operational view.} No prior work reports which model to trust for which attack, false-alarm rates, or what an operator should do.
    \item \textbf{Validity of the drift claim.} We test whether detected drift reflects traffic evolution or dataset assembly.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Threat model and placement}

The adversary is any device on the charging site's network, or a compromised vehicle on the charging cable, that sends reconnaissance or flooding traffic to a charging station. The detector is \emph{passive}: it receives a copy of the traffic from a mirrored switch port, classifies each completed flow and raises an alert. It does not block traffic; mitigation is left to people and policy, supported by the response guidance of Section~\ref{sec:system}.

\subsection{Classification task}

Each network flow is summarised by a feature vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ ($d = 60$ after correction) with a label $y \in \mathcal{Y}$, $|\mathcal{Y}| = K = 15$. A classifier $f:\mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathcal{Y}$ is learned from training flows. For operational use we also map each class to a response category,
\begin{equation}
c:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\{\text{DoS},\ \text{Recon},\ \text{Benign}\},
\label{eq:category}
\end{equation}
which groups the eight denial-of-service classes, the six reconnaissance classes and benign traffic.

\subsection{Metrics}

For class $k$ with precision $P_k$ and recall $R_k$, the per-class score and the headline metric are
\begin{equation}
F_{1,k} = \frac{2 P_k R_k}{P_k + R_k}, \qquad
\text{macro-}F_1 = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} F_{1,k}.
\label{eq:f1}
\end{equation}
Macro-$F_1$ weights every class equally, whereas accuracy and weighted $F_1$ weight classes by their support $n_k$ and are therefore dominated by the four large flood classes. Every result is reported as the mean $\bar{m}$ and sample standard deviation $s$ over $S = 3$ seeds,
\begin{equation}
\bar{m} = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} m_s, \qquad
s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{S-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\left(m_s - \bar{m}\right)^2 },
\label{eq:seeds}
\end{equation}
where each seed changes both the data split and the model initialisation.

\subsection{Corrected preprocessing}

Our pipeline (\col{src/data\_prep/preprocess.py}) proceeds as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Concatenation and labelling.} All 59 captures are concatenated and labelled from file names through a 19-alias map.
    \item \textbf{Identifier drops.} Flow IDs, IP, MAC and vendor identifiers, and sparse application-layer string fields are removed as environment-specific.
    \item \textbf{Leakage removal.} The six absolute timestamp columns (\col{*\_first\_seen\_ms}, \col{*\_last\_seen\_ms}) and \col{src\_port} are removed (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}); \col{dst\_port} is kept.
    \item \textbf{Constant columns} (\col{vlan\_id}, \col{tunnel\_id}) are removed.
    \item \textbf{Deduplication} on the reduced feature set.
    \item \textbf{Standardisation} of each feature $j$ with training mean $\mu_j$ and deviation $\sigma_j$: $z_j = (x_j - \mu_j)/\sigma_j$.
    \item \textbf{Stratified split} 70/15/15.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Leakage Analysis}
\label{sec:leakage}

\subsection{Finding 1: the label is recoverable from one timestamp}

Each attack was recorded in its own capture file at its own wall-clock time. An absolute timestamp therefore identifies the \emph{recording}, and through it the label, without describing the traffic. We test this with a single-feature probe: a decision tree $f_j$ of depth 25 trained on feature $j$ alone, scored by 3-fold cross-validated accuracy,
\begin{equation}
\text{Acc}(f_j) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{I}\!\left[f_j(x_{ij}) = y_i\right].
\label{eq:probe}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Finding 3: stream order inflates prequential accuracy}

A streaming learner is evaluated \emph{prequentially}: each instance is first predicted, then used for training. After $t$ instances,
\begin{equation}
\text{Acc}_t = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathbb{I}\!\left[f_{i-1}(\mathbf{x}_i) = y_i\right],
\label{eq:preq}
\end{equation}
where $f_{i-1}$ has seen only the first $i-1$ instances. This is sensitive to the order of the stream. Define the label autocorrelation
\begin{equation}
\rho = \Pr\!\left[y_t = y_{t-1}\right],
\label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
which is exactly the accuracy of the trivial rule ``predict the previous label''. In capture order each file holds one class, so labels change only at file boundaries: in the 1,921,182-flow training stream the label changes 54 times, giving $\rho = 0.99997$. For a uniformly shuffled stream with class proportions $p_k$,
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\text{shuffled}} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k^{2} = 0.117 .
\label{eq:rhoshuf}
\end{equation}

\label{tab:order}

\footnotesize

Table~\ref{tab:order} shows that in capture order ARF+ADWIN (0.9997) does not even beat the repeat-last rule (0.99997): its accuracy measures label autocorrelation, not learning. On the shuffled stream it scores 0.5585, well above both the repeat-last rule and the majority class (0.193); this is the meaningful figure, and it is 44 points lower.

\subsection{Finding 4: the drift detector finds file seams}

ADWIN \cite{b7} keeps a window $W$ of recent error indicators and declares drift when some split $W = W_0 W_1$ has sub-window means that differ by more than
\begin{equation}
\epsilon_{\text{cut}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2m}\ln\frac{4}{\delta'}}, \qquad
m = \frac{1}{1/n_0 + 1/n_1}, \quad \delta' = \frac{\delta}{n},
\label{eq:adwin}
\end{equation}
where $n_0, n_1$ are the sub-window sizes and $n = |W|$. In capture order, each file seam is an abrupt distribution change by construction. Let $b_1,\dots,b_B$ be the seam positions and $e_1,\dots,e_E$ the drift alarms. The distance of each alarm to its nearest seam and the number of aligned alarms are
\begin{equation}
d_i = \min_{j}\,\lvert e_i - b_j\rvert, \qquad
H = \sum_{i=1}^{E}\mathbb{I}[d_i \le w],
\label{eq:align}
\end{equation}
with $w = 100$ flows. Under a null model that places the $E$ alarms uniformly at random, $R = 2000$ draws give $H^{(r)}$ and the empirical significance
\begin{equation}
p = \frac{1 + \sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathbb{I}\!\left[H^{(r)} \ge H\right]}{1 + R}.
\label{eq:pvalue}
\end{equation}
In capture order $H = 42$ of $E = 43$ alarms (median distance 22 flows), against $2.2 \pm 1.5$ under the null, so $p < 0.0005$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:drift}). The detector is locating the seams of dataset assembly, not drift in charging traffic --- the property offered in \cite{b4} as the method's advantage.

\subsection{Finding 5: \col{src\_port} is a residual fingerprint}
\label{sec:srcport}

With rows and split held fixed and only \col{src\_port} removed (Table~\ref{tab:srcport}), the deeper Decision Tree loses more than twice as much as Random Forest, the signature of a memorisable fingerprint: extra depth buys finer port bands and nothing else. Without it the two converge, so the tree's earlier advantage was memorisation.

\label{sec:system}

Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarises the system from capture to response.

\subsection{Placement}

EVNet Sentinel sits where CICEVSE2024 was recorded: on the mirrored port of the switch that carries station, CSMS and vehicle traffic. It therefore sees every station's traffic, including ISO~15118 traffic from a vehicle, without being in its path or modifying station firmware.

\subsection{Models and prediction service}

Two pipelines are trained offline on the corrected features: static scikit-learn classifiers (Random Forest, Decision Tree, and SGD-trained Logistic Regression and linear SVM), and the River \cite{b9} implementation of ARF+ADWIN with the configuration of \cite{b4}. A FastAPI service loads every model and the matching scaler into a registry at start-up, tolerating individual load failures, and classifies one flow per request; the caller chooses the model by name.

\subsection{Response guidance}

Each detection maps to actions for three roles --- the station owner, the CSMS operator and the network analyst --- with a severity per attack. The guidance uses the per-attack reliability of Section~\ref{sec:perattack}: when the active model does not name the predicted attack reliably, it reports and acts on the category $c(\hat{y})$ of \eqref{eq:category} instead, with the severity range of that category, because acting on a confidently wrong name is worse than acting on a correct category.

\subsection{Front end}

A Next.js application is built entirely from exported results (no live inference), which keeps every displayed number reproducible. It provides an interactive landing page on which visitors aim an attack at a station and see the verdict the model actually returned for a real held-out flow, and can move the naming-confidence bar of Section~\ref{sec:confidence}; a simulation console that replays held-out flows from the device that really sent them to the station they really hit (Table~\ref{tab:routes}), with scripted multi-attack campaigns and an incident log; and a dashboard with the findings, per-attack results and figures of this paper.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Models}

Random Forest uses 100 trees of maximum depth 15; Decision Tree a maximum depth of 20; Logistic Regression and the linear SVM are trained by stochastic gradient descent with log and hinge loss. All four use balanced class weights,
\begin{equation}
w_k = \frac{N}{K\, n_k},
\label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
so that a misclassified flow of a rare class costs as much in total as one of a common class. ARF \cite{b6} uses 20 trees, \col{max\_features} = 0.5, a grace period of 30 and ADWIN with $\delta = 0.002$, as in \cite{b4}.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

Every headline number follows \eqref{eq:seeds} over seeds 42, 1337 and 2024. With Benign at 81 flows and ICMP fragmentation at 28, the split is the dominant source of run-to-run variation, which a single seed hides. Macro-$F_1$ \eqref{eq:f1} is the headline: on identical predictions the corrected Random Forest scores 0.868 accuracy but 0.558 macro-$F_1$.

\subsection{Per-attack test harness}

A single score per model hides which attacks it handles. For each attack class $a$, model $f$ and seed, we draw $T = 30$ batches $\mathcal{B}$ of $n = 50$ held-out flows of class $a$ and score each batch at three levels:
\begin{align}
D &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}} \mathbb{I}[\hat{y}_i \ne \text{Benign}], \nonumber\\
C &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}} \mathbb{I}[c(\hat{y}_i) = c(a)], \qquad
E = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}} \mathbb{I}[\hat{y}_i = a],
\label{eq:levels}
\end{align}
the \emph{detected}, \emph{category} and \emph{exact} rates, with $E \le C \le D$. Each rate is reported as its mean over trials with a 95\% interval $\bar{r} \pm 1.96\, s_r/\sqrt{T}$. A model's verdict for an attack follows a ladder with reliability threshold $\tau = 0.9$: \emph{identified} if $E \ge \tau$, else \emph{category} if $C \ge \tau$, else \emph{detected} if $D \ge \tau$, else \emph{missed}. Benign traffic is scored by its false-alarm rate
\begin{equation}
\text{FA} = \frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{B}_0\rvert}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_0}\mathbb{I}[\hat{y}_i \ne \text{Benign}],
\label{eq:fa}
\end{equation}
over the benign flows $\mathcal{B}_0$. Mixed campaigns send four attacks at once (25 flows each, 30 trials). The harness fails if a regression gate is crossed: each flood named exactly at $\ge 0.95$, each attack detected at $\ge 0.95$, and each scan categorised at $\ge 0.85$ by at least one model.

\subsection{Per-feature separability}

To ask whether a ceiling lies in the data or in the models, we measure, inside one category $g$ (DoS or reconnaissance), how much of the uncertainty about the class each feature $X_j$ removes on its own:
\begin{equation}
S_j^{(g)} = \frac{I\!\left(X_j;\,Y \mid c(Y)=g\right)}{H\!\left(Y \mid c(Y)=g\right)}, \quad
H(Y) = -\sum_k p_k \log p_k,
\label{eq:sep}
\end{equation}
where $I$ is estimated with a $k$-nearest-neighbour mutual-information estimator on 6,000 held-out flows per category. $S_j = 1$ means feature $j$ alone determines the class; $S_j = 0$ means it carries no information about it. Unlike permutation importance, \eqref{eq:sep} does not depend on any model.

\subsection{Confidence-gated naming}

Given class probabilities $\hat{p}(k \mid \mathbf{x})$, the model names the attack only when its top probability reaches a bar $t$, and otherwise reports only the category:
\begin{equation}
\text{output}(\mathbf{x}) =
\begin{cases}
\argmax_k \hat{p}(k\mid\mathbf{x}) & \text{if } \max_k \hat{p}(k\mid\mathbf{x}) \ge t,\\
c\!\left(\argmax_k \hat{p}(k\mid\mathbf{x})\right) & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
Over attack flows this splits outcomes into the shares named correctly $R(t)$, named wrongly $W(t)$, and reported by category only $H(t) = 1 - R(t) - W(t)$. Raising $t$ trades naming coverage for correctness.

\subsection{Choice of primary detector}

Counting per-attack wins would reward a model for being least wrong on scans it rarely names. We instead take the models whose mean exact rate $\bar{E}_f$ over well-sampled attacks is within 0.02 of the best, and among them choose the one with the fewest false alarms:
\begin{equation}
f^{\star} = \argmin_{f\,:\,\bar{E}_f \,\ge\, \max_g \bar{E}_g - 0.02} \text{FA}_f .
\label{eq:primary}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Why the scans cannot be separated}
\label{sec:why}

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering

The errors are structured (Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion}). First, \emph{scans collapse into one scan}: Random Forest labels 32--48\% of every other scan as OS fingerprinting, while Logistic Regression absorbs them into TCP port scan instead. Which class becomes the sink depends on the model; that a sink exists does not. Second, \emph{one confusion crosses the category boundary}: 8--11\% of every scan is labelled Slowloris by Random Forest (3\% by Logistic Regression), because both hold long-lived, near-idle connections. This is almost all of the error between reconnaissance and DoS.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Label noise.} Labels come from capture file names, so background traffic recorded during an attack carries the attack label; part of the reconnaissance confusion may be such flows, which no detector could name correctly.
    \item \textbf{Benign sparsity.} Only 81 benign flows exist. Binary attack-versus-benign classification is answered by always predicting ``attack'' and is excluded, and false-alarm rates are indicative.
    \item \textbf{Random split.} Headline results use a stratified random split for comparability; deduplication removes exact repeats but not near-duplicates from one capture on both sides. Grouped and temporal splits are implemented but not reported here.
    \item \textbf{One testbed.} Two stations, one CSMS and standard tools at default settings; a paced or disguised scan changes exactly the timing features the detector relies on.
    \item \textbf{Untested responses and unused modalities.} The response guidance is standard practice and was not itself measured; CICEVSE2024's host-event and power traces, which may separate the scans, are not used.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The high accuracies reported for ML-based intrusion detection on CICEVSE2024 are largely an artefact of how the dataset was assembled: one capture timestamp beats the published model, streaming accuracy in capture order is matched by repeating the previous label, and the drift detector fires on the seams between capture files. With these effects removed, volumetric floods remain trivially separable while the six nmap scan modes are not, and per-feature mutual information shows that this ceiling lies in the flow statistics, not in model capacity. Operationally, no attack goes unnoticed; Random Forest names attacks as well as any model while raising the fewest false alarms; and requiring confidence before naming turns most wrong scan names into correct category alerts.

Future work should (a)~model the packet sequence within a flow, which flow aggregation discards and which may separate the scan modes; (b)~fuse host-event and power data; and (c)~evaluate sequence models on grouped splits against the repeat-last baseline of \eqref{eq:rho}, which any model on an ordered stream must beat to show learning.
